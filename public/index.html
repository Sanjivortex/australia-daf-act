Australia's Digital Assets Framework Act: What It Means for Tokeniser

The Act starts on 8 April 2027. A business that holds digital tokens for customers, or holds an asset and issues one redeemable token for it, will then generally need an Australian financial services licence, and existing platforms have until 7 October 2027 to apply.[1][9] This guide shows where that line falls, when each step happens, what changes for issuers who tokenise through Tokeniser, and where Redbelly's compliance design fits and where it is still open.

01

The Act on one page

It adds two new financial products to the Corporations Act and puts the businesses that run them under ASIC licensing.

The law says

The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 is Act No. 38 of 2026. It amends the Corporations Act 2001 and the Australian Securities and Investments Commission Act 2001.[1]

The Bill was introduced on 26 November 2025, passed both Houses on 1 April 2026 and received Royal Assent on 8 April 2026.[3][1]

It is not a stand-alone crypto law. The two new platforms are regulated under the existing Australian financial services licence framework, with core duties such as acting efficiently, honestly and fairly, not misleading customers, explaining how assets are held, and offering dispute resolution.[4]

[1]DAF Acttitle page; s 3; assent date

[3]Parliament bill pageprogress of bill

Why it exists

The Government said when it introduced the Bill: "Currently, businesses can hold unlimited client digital assets without financial law safeguards. Recent collapses overseas have shown the consequences of inadequate oversight."[4]

ASIC says the Act addresses "regulatory gaps and consequent uncertainty" for digital assets and some of the infrastructure behind them, and aims to foster innovation, competition and growth.[9] ASIC's stated principle for putting it into practice is "same activity, same risk, same regulatory outcome".[8]

Five terms that carry the Act

The law says

Digital token

s 761GB

An electronic record someone can control in fact: they can transfer it, stop others transferring it, and show they can do both. Practical control matters, not legal title.[1]

Digital asset platform

s 761GC

An arrangement where an operator possesses digital tokens for, or on behalf of, a client. An exchange or custodian holding customers' tokens is the plain example.[1]

Tokenised custody platform

s 761GD

The operator holds an asset other than money, creates one token for each asset, and the token gives a right to redeem it or direct its delivery. Something that fits both definitions is a TCP.[1]

Public digital token infrastructure

s 9E

Software or hardware for digital tokens whose protocols are open and non-discretionary, let any person contribute to the network, and do not rely on any one critical participant. Regulations can switch the test off or name infrastructure that counts.[1]

Wrapped token

s 765E

A token that gives a right to redeem an underlying asset. That redemption right is ignored when deciding if the token is a financial product, so it broadly takes the character of the asset behind it.[1][10]

Two products, not one

RG 000.20

A platform is a financial product separate from anything held through it. Duties can apply to the platform and, separately, to a token on it that is itself a financial product, such as a tokenised share.[10]

02

The licensing line

Running a platform needs a licence. Some small, incidental or infrastructure activity does not.

Who needs a licence
  • Platform operators. From 8 October 2027, operators of digital asset platforms (DAPs) and tokenised custody platforms (TCPs), and some other providers of related services, need an AFS licence with the right authorisation unless an exemption applies.[9]
  • People around the platform. Dealing in a platform other than by issuing it, or giving advice about one, also needs a licence.[9]
  • A corporation, not a person. These services must be provided by a "constitutionally-covered corporation", which broadly means a trading, financial or foreign corporation. An individual or partnership providing them by phone, internet or similar means breaches a civil penalty provision.[1][2]
  • Platform duties on top of the usual ones. Licensed operators must follow ASIC's asset-holding standards and transactional and settlement standards, and keep platform rules with set contents, including transparent and non-discriminatory eligibility criteria. Before issuing a platform to a retail client they must give a DAP/TCP Guide and have a voting policy, and they must pass on communications about underlying assets when a client asks.[1]
  • Product design. An issuer that must prepare a DAP/TCP Guide must also make a target market determination for the platform.[1][10]

Outside the licence

The law says

  • Small DAPsDAPs only, not TCPs

    All four must hold: no financial products on any DAP run by the operator's closely-related group; no more than $10 million in transactions through those platforms in the last 12 months; whenever a token becomes a client's asset, that client's total entry value is no more than $5,000; and a notice has been lodged with ASIC.[1]

    Regulations can raise the amounts, and ASIC's draft guide says the value tests will rise with inflation. The platform is still a DAP.[1][10]

  • Incidental mentions and arranging

    Only telling someone a platform exists, or arranging for them to use one, in the ordinary course of business, where that is not a significant part of the business.[1]

  • Public digital token infrastructure

    Not a managed investment scheme and not a financial product. Taking part in consensus on it, such as validating, is not a financial service and is not a clearing and settlement facility.[1]

  • Custodial staking on a licensed platform

    Where the platform issuer holds an AFS licence to issue the platform, the staking part is not a separate scheme or financial product.[1][10]

  • Technology only

    ASIC's draft guide says an arrangement may not be a DAP where it only provides technology or infrastructure to an operator and does not possess tokens for the operator's clients, including some multi-party computation (MPC) key set-ups under expected regulations.[10]

  • Proposed in regulations

    Sub-custodian, overseas issuer and client, overseas related body corporate or joint venture, and nominee company exemptions, all "subject to Government processes". An exemption does not stop a facility being a DAP or TCP.[10][9]

When another regime takes over

The law says
  • Managed investment schemes. A platform that is also a scheme is regulated as a scheme unless carve-outs apply: clients can redeem the assets, the operator acts only on client instructions, and the operator cannot materially set clients' rights. A TCP must also hold one class of asset, and its tokens can only be as divisible as the asset can be physically delivered.[1][10]
  • Markets and clearing. Where a facility meets the financial market or clearing and settlement facility definition for assets that are financial products, that regime applies rather than the DAP rules.[10]
  • The Minister. The Minister can declare a DAP to be a market or clearing facility, exempt one from those regimes, or ban specified financial products from going through specified platforms.[1]

If you hold tokens on a licensed platform

The law says

A guide before you join

Retail clients get a DAP/TCP Guide first. It must cover the platform's risks, who the custodians are, all fees, how your rights differ from holding the asset directly, and how to complain.[1]

Your votes, your call

The platform needs a voting policy. Where you can instruct, it votes only as you instruct; where you cannot, it does not vote.[1]

Papers on request

Communications sent to holders of an underlying asset must be passed on to you when you ask, including through a standing request.[1]

Rules that bind the operator

Platform rules have effect as a contract between the operator and each client.[1]

Client money in trust

ASIC's asset-holding standards must require money held under a platform, other than underlying assets, to be held in trust for clients.[1]

Somewhere to complain

The Government says licensed platforms must provide accessible dispute resolution and compensation if things go wrong.[4]

The calendar

The Act is law now and starts on 8 April 2027. Existing platforms then have six months to apply, and can keep operating while ASIC decides.

Date check

8 April or 9 April 2027?

8 April 2027Federal Register commencement column; ASIC CP 392 and 26-245MR (15 October 2026)
9 April 2027ASIC's roadmap (20 April 2026) and news item (4 May 2026)

The Act starts on "the day after the end of the period of 12 months beginning on the day this Act receives the Royal Assent".[1] The Register records that day as 8 April 2027, in a column that is expressly not part of the Act.[1] ASIC's April and May 2026 material said 9 April 2027.[5][6] Its October 2026 consultation says 8 April 2027, with 7 October 2027 as the last day to apply.[9][8] This guide uses ASIC's latest dates. Issuers on Tokeniser

For most issuers the deciding question is custody: who can move the tokens, and whether an asset is held behind each one.

Company says
  • Tokeniser describes itself as software for fund administration and investor portals that runs on Redbelly Network, and as "a Redbelly Network project".[11][16]
  • It says: "Products issued through Tokeniser operate under an Asset Issuer's existing licensing", and that eligibility checks (identity, KYC, AML, CTF, wholesale and sophisticated investor tests, and target market determinations) are programmed into each asset.[11]
  • It says it does not hold investors' private keys; the investor or a designated key manager does, and it offers key management and recovery options.[15]
  • It lists IQ-EQ as providing trust, registry and fund administration services, and "licence and service".[16]

[11]Tokeniser homehome page; FAQ

[16]Tokeniser aboutabout page; division of responsibility

[15]Tokeniser custody FAQcustody answer

The law says
  • Tokenising is not, by itself, what gets licensed. ASIC's draft guide: "A TCP is one way assets may be tokenised. Not every tokenisation arrangement is a TCP."[10]
  • Native fund units. Where each token represents a unit in a unit trust and the blockchain is the primary unit-holder register, ASIC's draft guide says the arrangement "would generally be a managed investment scheme rather than a TCP".[10]
  • Tokens over financial products. A token a TCP creates over an asset that is a financial product will generally be the same type of financial product as that asset.[10][1]
  • Raising money through a platform. A person offering securities through a DAP or TCP under a disclosure document lodged with ASIC must promptly tell the operator and each custodian if a supplementary or replacement document is lodged, if the person becomes prohibited from making offers under it, or if it is withdrawn. The document does not need to describe the platform.[1]
  • Interests created by the platform. Operators and promoters are exempt from the fundraising rules for equitable interests in securities that arise from holding them through the platform.[1]
  • Trading venues. A business may run a financial market for tokenised securities alongside a DAP for tokens that are not financial products, and each part is assessed on its own.[10]

Analysis

Six things to settle before 8 April 2027

  1. List every party that holds keys or can move investors' tokens, including recovery and back-up arrangements.
  2. Write down, with reasons, whether each product and service is a DAP, a TCP, a scheme, a market, or none of these.
  3. Compare your AFS licence authorisations with ASIC's DAP and TCP authorisations once they are final.
  4. Record who configures eligibility rules and who signs them off. Tokeniser says the fund manager stays responsible for that and remains the AML/CTF reporting entity.[14]
  5. Read CP 392 and consider a submission by 27 November 2026.[8]
  6. Get advice from an Australian financial services lawyer before relying on any classification.

Redbelly and the rules

Redbelly's identity and eligibility tools line up with the parts of the law about access and records. Whether Redbelly counts as public digital token infrastructure is still open.

Company says
  • Redbelly's documentation calls Receptor its "native digital identity and verifiable credential protocol", which "facilitates network access" and helps use cases set eligibility criteria and users prove they meet them. It uses the W3C verifiable credential data model.[18]
  • The Eligibility Kit lets asset issuers check investor eligibility with zero-knowledge proofs, without the investor revealing the underlying personal data.[19] Its credential types include an AML/CTF credential and an Australian sophisticated or wholesale investor credential.[20]
  • Individual onboarding includes wallet authentication, KYC verification and on-chain permission set-up.[21] Business onboarding (KYB) is provided by Averer.[22]
  • Redbelly says AML, CTF and Travel Rule requirements "can be applied as needed".[17]

Side by side

Eligibility rules

Lines up
Law

Platform rules must include "transparent and non-discriminatory eligibility criteria for identifying who can become a client".[1]

Described

Criteria written as credential queries; Tokeniser says every transfer is checked against the asset's rules.[20][11]

Analysis

A good mechanism for applying criteria the same way every time. Writing criteria that are transparent and non-discriminatory stays the operator's job.

Records and reconciliation

Partly
Law

ASIC can set asset-holding standards on record-keeping, reconciliation and reporting.[1]

Described

Tokeniser says every check, transfer and rejection is recorded on-chain and in a compliance log.[14]

Analysis

A shared ledger should help with reconciliation. Whether it meets the standards depends on the final text, planned for March 2027.

Holding client assets

Depends
Law

Standards are expected to cover holding on trust, segregation and withdrawal rights, and must not ban one-wallet holding where internal records identify each client's tokens.[5][1]

Described

Tokeniser says investors hold tokens in their own wallets or through a designated key manager.[15]

Analysis

True self-custody may keep the DAP rules out entirely. Where a key manager can move tokens, the standards may apply to that party. See set-ups A to C.

Retail guide and target market

Lines up
Law

A DAP/TCP Guide before issuing to a retail client, and a target market determination where a Guide is required.[1][10]

Described

An Australian sophisticated or wholesale investor credential; Tokeniser says target market determinations are enforced at transfer.[20][12]

Analysis

Supports gating who can hold an asset. It does not write the Guide or the determination; those are still needed.

Settlement disclosure

Partly
Law

Platform rules must set out the settlement method, who bears counterparty and operational risk, and how acquisitions are recorded in any legal or technical register.[1]

Described

Deterministic finality, described as final "the moment it completes".[12]

Analysis

Clear finality should make the method easier to describe. The disclosure still has to be written and kept accurate.

AML/CTF

Supports
Law

The DAF Act itself contains no anti-money laundering rules. Its eligibility, conduct and disclosure duties fall on the licensed operator.[1]

Described

KYC in onboarding; Tokeniser says the fund manager remains the AML/CTF reporting entity.[21][14]

Analysis

Tools help the responsible entity meet its duties. They do not move those duties onto the network.

Voting and communications

Not addressed
Law

A voting policy that votes only on client instructions where clients can instruct, and communications about underlying assets passed on request.[1]

Described

No specific feature found in the sources reviewed.

Analysis

A gap to ask about, not to assume either way.

Pools and lending

Open
Law

Public infrastructure is outside the scheme and financial product definitions, and consensus is outside financial services and clearing.[1] The market regime applies where a facility meets the market definition for financial products.[10]

Described

Tokeniser says automated market maker liquidity and lending sit in an "exempt protocol layer", citing the Explanatory Memorandum.[13]

Analysis

The writer found no express carve-out of this infrastructure from the "financial market" definition in the Act. Whether a given pool is itself public infrastructure, and whether trading tokenised financial products through it is operating a market, depends on facts. ASIC intends to update INFO 225, which covers how the Act treats public digital token infrastructure, before finalising its guide in early 2027.[10] The Explanatory Memorandum passages Tokeniser cites were not checked for this guide.

Is Redbelly public digital token infrastructure?

Open
Law

Three tests: open, non-discretionary protocols; any person may contribute; no critical participant. Regulations can exclude a protocol or name infrastructure that counts.[1]

Described

Tokeniser says Redbelly is "designed to meet the three statutory tests".[12] Redbelly's own docs say the issuers who provide network access are accredited by Redbelly[18], node operators are "successful applicants" who receive an invitation[23], operators must stake RBNT and Redbelly may slash misbehaving participants[25], and governors must give Redbelly notice of planned downtime.[24]

Analysis

No ASIC guidance, court decision or regulation was found that decides this. Invitation-based node operation and Redbelly's role in accrediting network-access issuers raise questions under test (b), "any person" may contribute, and test (c), no critical participant. Tokeniser's own page says a protocol with a privileged role needs to evidence its design against test (c).[13]

Bottom line
  • The closest match is on who may access a product and keeping records.
  • The law puts duties on licensees, issuers and AML/CTF reporting entities, not on software. Tools help those people meet their duties. They do not take the duties over.
  • The biggest open questions are about classification: whether Redbelly is public digital token infrastructure, who possesses tokens under a given key set-up, and when secondary trading needs a market licence.
  • Nothing here says Redbelly, Tokeniser or any issuer is licensed, exempt or compliant. Most of the regime has not started.

What comes next

Five developments that will turn open questions into answers.

The law says
  • 27 November 2026: CP 392 submissions close.[8]
  • March 2027: planned publication of ASIC's final standards and guidance.[9]
  • Regulations: several exemptions, and the treatment of MPC key set-ups, depend on regulations still "subject to Government processes".[10]
  • INFO 225: ASIC intends to update it, including on public digital token infrastructure, wrapped tokens and custodial staking, before finalising its guide in early 2027.[10]
  • Payments reforms: some stablecoins will be regulated separately, as tokenised stored value facilities.[9]

Analysis

What would settle the Redbelly question. One of three things: ASIC guidance on how the three tests apply to permissioned networks; a regulation that names or excludes the protocol under s 9E; or a published, evidence-based assessment of Redbelly's protocol against each test that an adviser can rely on. The first two are for Government and ASIC. The third is something the community can ask for now.

Terms

Glossary
AFS licence (AFSL)
Australian financial services licence, issued by ASIC. DAP and TCP operators need one with the right authorisation unless exempt.[9]
ASIC
Australian Securities and Investments Commission, which licenses and supervises these platforms and enforces the law.[5]
Royal Assent
The step that turns a passed Bill into an Act. For this Act, 8 April 2026.[1]
Commencement
The day the rules start to operate: 8 April 2027 on ASIC's latest statement.[8]
Transition period
The six-month period starting on commencement.[1]
Platform rules
The operator's rules for a DAP or TCP, which have effect as a contract with each client.[1]
DAP/TCP Guide
The disclosure document given to a retail client before a platform is issued.[1]
Constitutionally-covered corporation
A corporation within s 51(xx) of the Constitution (foreign corporations, and trading or financial corporations formed in Australia), or a body that is a corporation under the Corporations Act as originally enacted.[1][2]
Managed investment scheme
A pooled investment arrangement regulated under Chapter 5C of the Corporations Act. Some platforms are carved out of the definition.[10]
Verifiable credential
A credential, such as proof of passing KYC, that an issuer gives a holder and a verifier can check. Receptor has issuer, holder and verifier modules.[18]
Zero-knowledge proof
A way to prove a statement, such as "is a wholesale investor", without revealing the data behind it.[19]
TMD
Target market determination, required under the design and distribution obligations.[10]
