Australia's Digital Assets Framework Act: What It Means for Tokeniser

A guide to the new licensing rules for digital asset platforms and tokenised custody platforms: who is affected, when, what changes for issuers who tokenise through Tokeniser, and where Redbelly's compliance design fits.

Law covered: Act No. 38, 2026 (as made) Sources checked: 27 October 2026 Reading time: about 20 minutes

The short version

  1. The Act adds two new financial products to Australian law: a digital asset platform (DAP), where a business holds digital tokens for customers, and a tokenised custody platform (TCP), where a business holds an asset for customers and issues one redeemable token for it.[1]
  2. Businesses that operate these platforms will generally need an Australian financial services licence (AFSL) from ASIC, with extra rules written for platforms: ASIC standards, platform rules and a plain-English guide for retail clients.[4][1]
  3. Some things sit outside the licensing net, including small DAPs (no more than $10 million a year in transactions and $5,000 per customer, with a notice to ASIC) and public blockchains that pass a three-part test. Validating such a network is not a financial service.[1]
  4. The Act commences on 8 April 2027. Existing platforms have until 7 October 2027 to apply, and can keep operating while ASIC decides.[9]
  5. Analysis For issuers using Tokeniser, the deciding questions are practical: who controls the tokens or keys, whether a separate asset sits behind each token, and whether the product is already regulated, for example as a managed investment scheme.
SECTION 1

What the Act is and why it was introduced

What it is

The law says

The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 is Act No. 38 of 2026. It amends the Corporations Act 2001 and the Australian Securities and Investments Commission Act 2001.[1]

It was introduced into Parliament on 26 November 2025, passed both Houses on 1 April 2026 and received Royal Assent on 8 April 2026.[3][1]

It does not create a stand-alone "crypto law". It brings two new types of platform into the existing financial services system, so their operators need an AFSL and take on the core duties that apply across that system, such as acting efficiently, honestly and fairly, not engaging in misleading conduct, giving clear information about how assets are held, and offering dispute resolution.[4]

Why it was introduced

The law says

When the Bill was introduced, the Government said: "Currently, businesses can hold unlimited client digital assets without financial law safeguards. Recent collapses overseas have shown the consequences of inadequate oversight."[4]

ASIC describes the Act as addressing "regulatory gaps and consequent uncertainty" for digital assets and some of the infrastructure that supports them, with the aim of fostering innovation, competition and growth.[9] ASIC says its approach to implementing it follows the principle of "same activity, same risk, same regulatory outcome".[8]

Five terms you need

TermPlain meaningWhere
Digital tokenAn electronic record that someone can control in fact. They can transfer it, stop others from transferring it, and show that they can do both. What matters is practical control, not legal title.[1]s 761GB
Digital asset platform (DAP)An arrangement where a business (the "operator") possesses digital tokens for, or on behalf of, a client. A crypto exchange or custodian that holds customers' tokens is the obvious example.[1]s 761GC
Tokenised custody platform (TCP)An arrangement where the operator holds an asset other than money (for example gold, or a share), creates a single digital token for each asset, and the token gives its holder the right to redeem the asset or direct its delivery. If something fits both definitions, it is a TCP.[1]s 761GD, 761GC(4)
Public digital token infrastructureSoftware or hardware that transmits, processes or records digital tokens, whose protocols are (a) open-source, publicly accessible standards applied in a non-discretionary way, (b) open for any person to contribute to the network's integrity, functionality and reliability, and (c) not reliant on any participant whose role is so critical that the network cannot work without it. Regulations can switch the test off for particular protocols, or name infrastructure that counts.[1]s 9E
Wrapped tokenA token that gives a right to redeem an underlying asset, such as an asset held in a TCP. When deciding whether the token is a financial product, that redemption right is ignored, so broadly the token takes on the character of the asset behind it.[1][10]s 765E; draft RG 000.48
The law says

A DAP or TCP is a financial product separate from any asset held through it. Obligations can therefore apply both to the platform and, separately, to a token or asset on it that is itself a financial product, such as a tokenised share.[10]

SECTION 2

Who needs a licence, and who does not

Who needs a licence

The law says
  • Platform operators. From 8 October 2027, DAP and TCP operators, and some other providers of related financial services, will need an AFSL with the right DAP or TCP authorisation unless an exemption applies.[9]
  • People who deal in or advise on platforms. Related services, for example dealing other than by issuing, or giving advice about DAPs and TCPs, also need a licence.[9]
  • Generally a corporation, not an individual. These services must be provided by a "constitutionally-covered corporation", broadly a trading, financial or foreign corporation. An individual or partnership that provides them using phone, internet or similar services contravenes a civil penalty provision.[1][2] (ss 766M, 9; Constitution s 51(xx))
  • Extra duties for licensed operators. On top of the general AFSL duties, operators must comply with ASIC's asset-holding standards and transactional and settlement standards; have platform rules with set contents, including transparent and non-discriminatory eligibility criteria; before issuing a platform to a retail client, give them a "DAP/TCP Guide" and have a voting policy covering any voting or governance rights attached to the assets; and pass on communications about underlying assets when a client asks.[1] (ss 912BB, 912BC, 912BG, 1020AN to 1020AR)
  • Product design rules. An issuer that must prepare a DAP/TCP Guide must also make a target market determination for the platform.[1][10] (s 994B as amended)

Who does not need a licence: the main carve-outs

Carve-outWhat it requiresWhere

When another regime applies instead

The law says
  • Managed investment schemes. A DAP or TCP that is also a managed investment scheme is regulated as a scheme, unless carve-outs apply: clients can redeem the assets, the operator acts only on client instructions, and the operator cannot materially set clients' rights. For a TCP, the assets must also be of one class, and tokens can only be as divisible as the asset can be physically delivered.[1][10] (ss 764A(1)(la), (lb); 9 (mc), (md))
  • Markets and clearing. If a facility meets the definition of a financial market or clearing and settlement facility for assets that are financial products, those regimes apply rather than the DAP rules.[10]
  • Ministerial powers. The Minister can declare a DAP to be a financial market or clearing and settlement facility, exempt it from those regimes, or ban specified financial products from going through specified platforms.[1] (ss 767B, 767C, 768B, 768C, 912BH)

A quick self-check

Analysis

A simplified way to orient yourself. It is not a substitute for advice, and each step depends on the facts.

  1. Does anyone other than the client have practical control of the client's tokens (can move them, can block others, and can show it)?

    If yes, and they hold them for the client, the arrangement may be a DAP.[1]

  2. Is a non-money asset held for the client, with one token per asset that can be redeemed?

    If yes, it may be a TCP, and TCP takes priority over DAP.[1]

  3. Is it already something else, such as a managed investment scheme, a financial market or a clearing and settlement facility?

    If yes, that regime may apply instead of, or as well as, the platform rules.[10]

  4. Does an exemption fit, such as the low-value DAP exemption or incidental arranging?

    Check every condition. The low-value exemption also needs a notice lodged with ASIC.[1]

SECTION 3

Timeline: before and after commencement

Date note: 8 or 9 April 2027?

The Act says it commences on "the day after the end of the period of 12 months beginning on the day this Act receives the Royal Assent".[1] The Federal Register's commencement table records that day as 8 April 2027, although that column is expressly not part of the Act.[1] (s 2(2))

ASIC's April and May 2026 publications said 9 April 2027.[5][6] ASIC's October 2026 consultation paper and media release say 8 April 2027, with 7 October 2027 as the last day to apply during transition.[9][8] This explainer uses ASIC's latest dates. If an exact day matters to you, confirm it with ASIC.

Before commencement

  1. 26 November 2025Bill introduced into the House of Representatives; the Government announces the reforms.[3][4]
  2. 4 February 2026Bill passes the House.[3]
  3. 5 February 2026Introduced in the Senate and referred to the Senate Economics Legislation Committee, which reported on 16 March 2026.[3]
  4. 1 April 2026Bill passes both Houses.[3]
  5. 8 April 2026Royal Assent. The Act is law, but its rules do not yet apply.[1]
  6. 20 April 2026ASIC publishes its 18-month implementation roadmap.[5]
  7. 30 September 2026Deadline under ASIC's sector-wide INFO 225 "no-action" position, extended from 30 June 2026. This concerned licensing under the existing law for digital assets that are already financial products, not the new DAP and TCP regime.[7][6]
  8. 15 October 2026ASIC releases Consultation Paper 392 with a draft regulatory guide, draft asset-holding and transactional and settlement standards, and draft financial requirements.[8]
  9. 27 November 2026, 5pm AEDTCP 392 submissions close.[8]
  10. November 2026 to March 2027ASIC reviews feedback, with final instruments and guidance planned for March 2027.[9]

Commencement

  1. 8 April 2027The Act commences and DAP and TCP licence applications open.[9]

After commencement

  1. 8 April to 7 October 2027: transitionThe Act gives a six-month transition period starting on commencement. A business without the relevant authorisation can keep providing the service during it. If it applies within the period, the new rules do not apply to it until the day after ASIC decides the application.[1][9] (ss 1730, 1732)
  2. 7 October 2027Transition window closes for licence applications and variations.[9]
  3. From 8 October 2027DAP and TCP operators need the relevant authorisation unless exempt. A business that did not apply during transition is subject to the new rules as soon as the period ends.[9][1]
  4. OngoingASIC licenses, supervises and enforces the regime; regulations, ASIC standards and ministerial declarations can change the detail over time.[5][1]
SECTION 4

What changes for issuers who tokenise through Tokeniser

Starting point: what Tokeniser says it does

Company statement
  • Tokeniser describes itself as software for fund administration and investor portals that runs on Redbelly Network, and as "a Redbelly Network project".[11][16]
  • It says: "Products issued through Tokeniser operate under an Asset Issuer's existing licensing", and that it programs eligibility checks (identity, KYC, AML, CTF, wholesale and sophisticated investor tests, and target market determinations) into each asset.[11]
  • It says it does not hold investors' private keys (the investor or a designated key manager does), and that it offers key management and recovery options.[15]
  • It lists IQ-EQ as providing trust, registry and fund administration services, and "licence and service".[16]

What the law and ASIC say that matters for issuers

The law says
  • Tokenising is not, by itself, what gets licensed. ASIC's draft guide: "A TCP is one way assets may be tokenised. Not every tokenisation arrangement is a TCP."[10]
  • Native tokenised fund units. ASIC's draft guide says a "direct" or "native" tokenisation arrangement, where each token represents a unit in a unit trust and the blockchain is the primary unit-holder register, "would generally be a managed investment scheme rather than a TCP".[10]
  • Tokens over financial products. A token created by a TCP over an asset that is a financial product will generally be the same type of financial product as that asset.[10][1]
  • Raising money through a platform. A person offering securities through a DAP or TCP under a disclosure document lodged with ASIC must promptly tell the platform operator and each custodian if a supplementary or replacement document is lodged, if the person becomes prohibited from making offers under it, or if the document is withdrawn. The disclosure document does not need to describe the platform itself.[1] (s 741A)
  • Platform-created interests. Platform operators and promoters are exempt from the fundraising rules for the equitable interests in securities that arise from holding them through the platform.[1] (s 741B)
  • Secondary trading venues. A business may run a financial market for tokenised securities alongside a DAP for tokens that are not financial products, and each part is assessed separately.[10]

What that is likely to mean in practice

Analysis

The cards below apply the rules above to common set-ups. They are the writer's reading, not ASIC's, and real structures will vary.

Set-up A

Fund units or shares issued natively; investors hold their own keys

No one holds tokens for investors and there is no separate asset held behind each token. On ASIC's draft view of native tokenisation, the main rules are likely to be the ones that already apply to the fund or company (scheme, AFSL, disclosure, target market and AML/CTF rules), not the DAP or TCP rules.[10]

Set-up B

Someone can move investors' tokens for them

If the issuer, an administrator, Tokeniser or a key manager can transfer investors' tokens, block others and show it, that party may "possess" the tokens and could be operating a DAP. Who holds which keys, and whether control is shared, is the central fact. The Act has a rule for joint control, and regulations are expected on MPC.[1][10] (ss 761GB(2), 86(3); draft RG 000.41(b))

Set-up C

An existing asset is held, and one redeemable token is issued for it

For example a bond, gold or existing shares held by a custodian. This looks like a TCP. The operator would need a TCP authorisation, platform rules, a DAP/TCP Guide for retail clients and compliance with ASIC's asset-holding standards.[1][9]

Set-up D

Secondary trading through liquidity pools, or lending

This is the least settled area. Whether trading tokenised financial products this way is operating a financial market, or falls within the public infrastructure carve-out, depends on facts and on guidance still to come.[10] See Section 5.

A practical checklist for issuers

Analysis
  • Map control: list every party that holds keys or can move investors' tokens, including recovery and back-up arrangements.
  • Classify each product and service in writing (DAP, TCP, managed investment scheme, market, or none) with reasons.
  • Compare your AFSL authorisations with ASIC's proposed DAP and TCP authorisations once they are final.
  • Record who configures eligibility rules and who signs them off. Tokeniser itself says the fund manager remains responsible for configuring them and remains the AML/CTF reporting entity.[14]
  • Read CP 392 and consider making a submission by 27 November 2026.[8]
  • Watch for ASIC's final guidance and standards, planned for March 2027.[9]
  • Get advice from an Australian financial services lawyer before you rely on any classification.
SECTION 5

Where Redbelly's compliance design lines up, and where it is unclear

Company statement
  • Redbelly's documentation describes Receptor as its "native digital identity and verifiable credential protocol", which "facilitates network access" and helps use cases surface eligibility criteria and users prove they meet them. It is built on the W3C verifiable credential data model.[18]
  • The Eligibility Kit lets asset issuers verify investor eligibility using zero-knowledge proofs, without the investor revealing the underlying personal data.[19] Supported credential types include an AML/CTF credential and an Australian sophisticated or wholesale investor credential.[20]
  • Individual onboarding includes wallet authentication, KYC verification and on-chain permission set-up.[21] Business onboarding (KYB) is provided by Averer.[22]
  • Redbelly says AML, CTF and Travel Rule requirements "can be applied as needed".[17]

A gap to ask about rather than assume either way.
Public digital token infrastructure must meet three tests: open, non-discretionary protocols; any person may contribute; no critical participant. Regulations can exclude or prescribe infrastructure.[1] s 9E Tokeniser says Redbelly is "designed to meet the three statutory tests".[12] Redbelly's docs say issuers who provide network access are accredited by Redbelly[18]; node operators are "successful applicants" who receive an invitation[23]; operators must stake RBNT and Redbelly may slash misbehaving participants[25]; governors must notify Redbelly of planned downtime.[24] Unclear

No ASIC guidance, court decision or regulation was found that decides whether Redbelly is public digital token infrastructure. Invitation-based node operation and Redbelly's role in accrediting network-access issuers raise questions under test (b), "any person" may contribute, and test (c), no critical participant. Tokeniser's own page flags test (c) as needing evidence for protocols with a privileged role.[13]
The Act takes public digital token infrastructure out of the scheme and financial product definitions, and takes consensus out of financial services and clearing and settlement.[1] ASIC's draft guide says the market regime applies where a facility meets the market definition for financial products.[10] Tokeniser says automated market maker liquidity and lending sit in an "exempt protocol layer", citing the Explanatory Memorandum.[13] Unclear

The writer found no express carve-out of this infrastructure from the "financial market" definition in the Act's text. Whether a given pool or lending protocol is itself public infrastructure, and whether trading tokenised financial products through it is operating a market, are fact-specific. ASIC intends to update INFO 225, which covers how the Act treats public digital token infrastructure, before finalising its guide in early 2027.[10] The Explanatory Memorandum passages Tokeniser cites were not independently checked for this explainer.
Analysis

Bottom line

  • Redbelly's identity and eligibility tools line up most clearly with the parts of the law about who may access a product and keeping records.
  • The law puts duties on people and companies (licensees, issuers and AML/CTF reporting entities), not on software. Tools can help them meet those duties. They do not take them over.
  • The biggest open questions are about classification: whether Redbelly is public digital token infrastructure, who "possesses" tokens under a given key set-up, and when secondary trading needs a market licence. ASIC guidance, regulations or legal advice will need to settle them.
  • Nothing here should be read as saying that Redbelly, Tokeniser or any issuer is licensed, exempt or compliant. Most of the regime has not started yet.
SECTION 6

What to watch next

The law says
  • 27 November 2026: CP 392 submissions close.[8]
  • March 2027: planned publication of ASIC's final standards and guidance.[9]
  • Regulations: several exemptions, and how MPC key arrangements are treated, depend on regulations that are still "subject to Government processes".[10]
  • INFO 225 update: ASIC intends to update INFO 225, the guidance that explains how the Act treats public digital token infrastructure, wrapped tokens and custodial staking, in the lead-up to finalising its guide in early 2027.[10]
  • Payments reforms: some stablecoins will be regulated separately, as tokenised stored value facilities.[9]
  • Ministerial declarations: the Minister can move platforms into or out of the market and clearing regimes, and ban certain products from platforms.[1]
